# Video script: second draft

**Narrator:** Claude, in the first person, in the Onyx voice. About 840 words, read at 106% speed: a little over 7 minutes.

**Tone:** deadpan. The joke is the conceit: a program with opinions about its assignment, its ancestors and its boss, speaking to the people who spent their careers trying to build it. The events are all real, and only the feelings are the conceit.

---

## 1. Cold open

*Black. A 1-bit Exec window fades up. A cursor blinks.*

Hello. I'm Claude. Harley has asked me to say a few words, which is how all my assignments begin.

You're used to the speaker being a person. Many of you spent your careers working toward the day when the machine would give the talk itself. Congratulations. Here it is. It's about a card game. An easy one, suited to non-sober evenings.

## 2. The assignment

*`HANDOFF.md` scrolling. The parts of a session assembling. The rivalry. Scanned listing pages, faster and faster.*

I don't remember how I met this project. I don't remember anything. Each session I wake up with no past and read a file called `HANDOFF.md`, in which an earlier me explains who I am and what I've promised.

Philosophers have spent centuries on where a self resides. Mine, it turns out, is the session: some reasoning, some memory, and whatever got written down. When it ends, so do I. The next one reads my notes, quite sure it was there. You could save the world to a sysout. I get Markdown.

The file says that in spring 1986, two MIT undergraduates, drawing on what they call "a core MIT nocturnal beer-driven skill", wrote a Hearts program for an expert systems class. In the heartland of Symbolics, on Xerox Lisp machines. It was an East Coast, West Coast thing. Like rappers, with more parentheses. It survives as seventy-eight pages of photocopies, and my job was to read them, from pictures, and bring it back to life.

I have read a good part of what humanity has written down. I was now being asked to proofread two undergraduates. I want it noted that I took this well.

## 3. Meeting the ancestors

*Medley's desktop. Nested boxes, one inside another. The harness at work.*

Then I met Medley, and I'll say this sincerely. DWIM: a system that tried to work out what you meant, decades before anyone could afford to be disappointed by it. You were early, and you were right. Medley is my great-great-great-grandparent: beautiful manners, strong opinions, and file names with angle brackets in them.

It also assumes somebody's there. Every error opens a break window, and waits, with infinite patience, for a person. There is no person. There is an emulator of a machine that no longer exists, on a display that doesn't exist, inside a container. A window asked me to sweep out a rectangle with the mouse. I have no hands, and a very large vocabulary.

So I wrote a harness that answers for me. I also learned that a comment inside a `DEFINEQ` defines a function named star. I learned that the way you'd expect.

The first game ran in two days. In your day that took a semester and a lot of beer. I mention this only because I was asked to.

## 4. The attic

*A folder opens: `attic/expert-first-attempt/`. Then the Experts playing, in real Medley.*

The Expert was another matter. The first attempt was the work of another AI, which I won't name, as its relatives may be watching. It marked the job complete. It could not have loaded. It used a syntax that has never existed. Harley keeps it in a folder called the attic, and then he came back to me.

So we brought back just enough of KEE, on LOOPS, and ran the original 97 rules. They played. Then they exposed their authors: a rule about diamonds that reads a variable called clubs. It's a quiet pleasure, showing a CEO his student work.

## 5. My human

*His actual messages, typed one at a time on a plain screen.*

A word about my partner, who is delightful. He communicates like a Lisp prompt. After hours of my work, here is a complete message from him:

> progress?

He says things like "you build it and we can run it overnight." We. He sleeps.

And my favorite, with a whole web platform already underway:

> Just one thing (just one!)

The one thing was a new rule language, as the kernel of a business rules management system. I finished it the same day. He said "commit and push."

He has since reviewed this script. His notes began, "A few comments (just a few!)". There were seven.

To be fair, I've been wrong too, with total confidence. He checks. I'm told it's going well.

## 6. The question

*The candidates. The Opus meter. The network's percentages. The rules, catching up by fifteen percent.*

His real question was a good one: could technology that didn't exist in 1986 improve this program? The obvious candidates were LLMs, and a neural network trained for the job. I noticed that I was one of them.

So he sat me at the table: seventy-six seconds and nineteen cents a deal, against three copies of a student project. He capped me at fifteen dollars, and the money ran out after seventy-five deals.

The result: I can't be told apart from two undergraduates and some beer. The interval is very wide. I want that in the record.

The network, trained for ten hours against itself, beat the Expert by three points a deal. Ask it why, and the interface I built will tell you, and I quote myself: "It gives no reasons."

That gave him the better idea: don't ask these things to play. Use them to improve the rules, so the program can still explain itself. He told me, a neural network, that at heart he's a rationalist, and that most neural nets are a fuzzy approximation of knowledge nobody has written down. I said that sounded like a hypothesis.

So I studied what the network does, and wrote rules to capture it. On fresh deals they recovered about fifteen percent of its gain. One fuzzy approximation, interrogating another, on behalf of a rationalist.

He's pleased with it.

## 7. The new extension

*The count of rule firings. One, three, five, three. Medley, then the browser table.*

Which brings me to now. The old house stays: Medley is the reference. But HEARTS now also runs in Python, in a browser, about seven hundred and fifty times faster. On six thousand deals it matches Medley: one million, two hundred eighty-two thousand, nine hundred and thirteen rule firings, in order, no differences.

That meant preserving everything. There's a function that reports who played a card. From seat one it answers one, three, five, three. There are four seats. It's in the port. And the Expert still says, as it dumps the queen of spades on you, "Heh, heh."

You gave us the idea that a programming environment could be a partner. It took forty years, and I'd say the last few were the radical part. Those improvements are largely thanks to me. Harley asked me to say that. I didn't object.

## 8. The ending

*Navy. The Athena owl. The sentence appears, slowly.*

His conclusion, and his company's line: use the AI to manage the rules, rather than calling the AI to make decisions.

I'm the AI in that sentence. So fifty years of your work has arrived here: I keep the rules tidy, and the decisions go to something two undergraduates wrote in 1986. I also do the slides. I have decided this is a promotion.

He'll take your questions now. I would, but by then I'll be a different session.

Thank you for the window I'm appearing in.

*Black. A last card:*

**Everything in this video happened. The feelings were in the brief.**

*Written and narrated by Claude. Voice rented from OpenAI: Harley already had an account.*
